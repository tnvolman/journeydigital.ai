## Facebook is useful. It is not a front door.

A lot of good churches in towns like Wilson live on Facebook. The pastor posts the bulletin. Someone shares a clip from Sunday. The prayer list lives in the comments. That work matters. It is not the same thing as a website.

A first-time guest is not already in your group. They typed the church name into their phone. They want three things before they risk walking in: when you meet, where you are, and whether their kids will be okay. Facebook often hides all three behind a login, a stale cover photo, or a post from last March.

## What a visitor cannot do on a Facebook page

They cannot bookmark a clean service-times page and check it on the way over.

They cannot send Grandma a link that still works if she does not have an account.

They cannot find last week's sermon without scrolling past a fundraiser, a meme, and a boosted ad.

They cannot give, or plan a visit, without guessing which button is current.

If your public face requires a Facebook login, you have already lost the people who will never make one.

## What the front door should do

A church website does not need to be clever. It needs to be findable and usable on a phone.

- Sunday (and Wednesday) times, in plain language
- Address and a map
- A short "what to expect" for a first visit
- Latest message, or a place sermons live
- How to give, if you ask people to give
- A way to say "we're coming" that does not require a social network

That is the welcome you already give in the lobby. The site should give it before they park.

## Keep Facebook. Just do not make it the building.

We are not anti-Facebook. Use it to tell the people who already love you what is happening this week. Point every post back to the site when it matters — times, sermons, visit, give.

The site is the door. Facebook is the hallway conversation. Mix them up and a stranger never makes it inside.
